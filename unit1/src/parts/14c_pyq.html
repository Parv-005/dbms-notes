14.3 Data models — compare and recommend

End-sem 2023 (ITITC05) Q1(c)4 marks

Explain the key differences between the three main types of data models: hierarchical, network and relational. Provide examples of scenarios where each model is most suitable and discuss their advantages and limitations.

Show answer

Hierarchical: data as a tree of records, each child has one parent; relationships are pointers (1:N). Network: data as a graph; records linked by owner–member sets, a record may have many owners (M:N via link records). Relational: data as tables; relationships through common values (primary key – foreign key); queried declaratively with SQL.

Most suitable scenarios

  • Hierarchical: naturally tree-shaped data accessed top-down — organisation chart (CEO → managers → staff), file/folder directory, bill of materials, Windows registry, XML documents.
  • Network: many-to-many data with fixed, high-speed traversal — legacy airline reservation, telecom networks, supply chains (parts ↔ suppliers).
  • Relational: general business data with ad-hoc queries and integrity needs — banking, university, hospital, e-commerce orders.

Advantages / limitations: Hierarchical — simple, fast for 1:N top-down access, automatic parent–child integrity / cannot model M:N without redundancy, rigid access path, insert/delete anomalies, poor independence. Network — models M:N without duplication, flexible paths / complex, navigational record-at-a-time programming, restructuring breaks programs. Relational — simple tables, high data independence, powerful SQL, constraints, normalization, ACID / joins can be costly on huge data, rigid schema, harder horizontal scaling.

End-sem 2024 Q1(b)5 marks

A healthcare organization is deciding whether to use a hierarchical, network or relational data model to manage patient records. Analyze the key characteristics of each model, and recommend the most suitable model for managing complex relationships (such as patients, treatments and medical staff) effectively. Justify your choice.

Show answer

Relationships in the scenario: a patient sees many doctors and a doctor treats many patients (M:N); a treatment involves a patient, a doctor, nurses and medicines (many-to-many and multi-way); staff belong to departments (1:N). Queries are varied: a patient’s full history, a doctor’s schedule, all patients given drug X, billing summaries.

  • Hierarchical: e.g. Department → Doctor → Patient → Treatment. Because each child has one parent, a patient treated by three doctors must be stored three times (redundant, inconsistent medical history); “all doctors who treated patient P” needs a scan of the entire tree. Unsuitable.
  • Network: Patient and Doctor records linked by TREATMENT link records with two owners — M:N is possible without duplication. But every query must be written as pointer navigation, structure changes (adding a lab-test record type) require rewriting programs, and there is no ad-hoc query language. Workable but hard to maintain.
  • Relational: PATIENT(PID, …), DOCTOR(DID, …, DeptID), DEPARTMENT(DeptID, …), TREATMENT(TID, PID, DID, Date, Diagnosis), PRESCRIPTION(TID, MedID, Dose), MEDICINE(MedID, …). Every M:N is a table of foreign keys; any question is one SQL query.

Recommendation: relational model, because

  1. it represents all the M:N and multi-way relationships cleanly with no duplication of patient data (normalization);
  2. declarative SQL supports the many unforeseen queries doctors, administrators and auditors ask;
  3. integrity constraints (FK: a prescription must refer to an existing treatment; CHECK on dosage) protect safety-critical data;
  4. ACID transactions and recovery are essential for medical and billing records; views and GRANT protect patient privacy;
  5. high data independence — new tests, departments or fields can be added without rewriting applications; standard SQL tools and skills are widely available.

(Unstructured parts such as scan images or doctor notes may additionally be stored in a document/object store, but the core records should be relational.)

End-sem 2026 (summer) Q1(b)5 marks

A logistics company wants to manage information about warehouses, delivery vehicles, routes and shipments. Compare the hierarchical, network and relational data models, and recommend the most suitable model with proper justification.

Show answer

Relationships: a warehouse stores many shipments (1:N); a shipment may pass through several warehouses and a warehouse handles many shipments (M:N); a vehicle runs many routes and a route is served by many vehicles (M:N); a route connects many warehouses. The data is a network of many-to-many links, and queries are varied (where is shipment S? which vehicles are free on route R? load per warehouse today?).

  • Hierarchical (Warehouse → Vehicle → Shipment): a shipment passing through two warehouses or carried by two vehicles must be duplicated; queries like “all warehouses on route R” do not follow the tree. Not suitable.
  • Network: Warehouse, Vehicle, Route, Shipment records connected by set types (ROUTE_STOP, ASSIGNMENT link records) — M:N without duplication and fast fixed traversals; but programs navigate pointers, schema changes are costly, and ad-hoc tracking reports need new programs.
  • Relational: WAREHOUSE(WID, City, Capacity), VEHICLE(VID, Type, Capacity), ROUTE(RID, Source, Dest), ROUTE_STOP(RID, WID, Seq), SHIPMENT(SID, Weight, Src, Dst, Status), TRIP(TripID, VID, RID, Date), CARRIES(TripID, SID). All M:N are tables; every question is a join.

Recommendation: relational — it models all the M:N relationships without redundancy, supports real-time tracking and analytics through SQL, enforces integrity (a shipment cannot be assigned to a non-existent vehicle), handles concurrent updates from many depots with ACID transactions, and evolves easily. (For route optimisation over a road graph, a graph database could complement it.)

Mid-sem 2025 Q2(a)2 marks

A music streaming platform stores information about Songs, Artists and Playlists. Each playlist can have multiple songs, and each song can appear in multiple playlists. Compare how the hierarchical, network and relational models would represent this data, and explain why the relational model is preferred.

Show answer
  • Hierarchical: Playlist → Song (→ Artist). A song in 1000 playlists is stored 1000 times; renaming the song means 1000 updates; “playlists containing song S” needs a scan of all trees.
  • Network: Playlist and Song records joined by a PLAYLIST_ENTRY link record owned by both — no duplication, but queries navigate pointers and changes (adding position, added-on date) require program changes.
  • Relational: SONG(SongID, Title, ArtistID), ARTIST(ArtistID, Name), PLAYLIST(PID, Name, User), PLAYLIST_SONG(PID, SongID, Position) with foreign keys.

Relational is preferred: the M:N is a simple junction table with no duplication; any query (top songs in playlists, playlists of artist A) is one SQL statement; FKs prevent dangling entries; new attributes can be added without breaking apps (data independence).

Mid-sem 2024 Q1(b)2 marks

What are the potential drawbacks of using a network data model for complex queries in a database with many-to-many relationships, and how does the relational model address these challenges?

Show answer

Drawbacks of the network model: (i) queries are navigational — the programmer must follow owner/member pointers record-at-a-time (“find owner, get next member…”) and know the physical structure; (ii) each M:N needs extra link records and set types, making the schema complex; (iii) a new kind of query needs a new program; (iv) changing the structure breaks existing programs (little data independence); (v) no query optimiser — performance depends on the programmer’s chosen path.

Relational solution: M:N becomes a junction table of foreign keys; queries are declarative (SQL joins state what is wanted), so complex queries are short and ad hoc; the optimiser chooses the access path; relationships are by values, not pointers, giving physical and logical data independence.

End-sem 2025 Q1(b)5 marks

Discuss the terms “security of a database” and “integrity of a database” with the help of appropriate examples. Also, critically compare the relative advantages and disadvantages of a relational database over a hierarchical database.

Show answer

Security means protecting the database against unauthorised access, modification or destruction — intentional or accidental. Mechanisms: authentication (user/password), authorisation (privileges — GRANT SELECT ON marks TO student_role, REVOKE), views that hide columns, encryption, auditing. Example: in a bank, a clerk can read customer balances of their branch but cannot see employees’ salaries; a customer can see only their own account.

Integrity means the data is accurate, valid and consistent — protecting it from invalid changes by authorised users. Mechanisms: integrity constraints — domain (data type, CHECK (age BETWEEN 18 AND 120)), NOT NULL, entity integrity (primary key unique and not null), referential integrity (foreign keys), transactions. Example: an ENROLLED row cannot refer to a roll number that is not in STUDENT; a balance cannot become negative.

Difference in one line: security keeps the wrong users out; integrity keeps wrong data out.

Relational vs hierarchical

Relational — advantages over hierarchicalRelational — disadvantages compared to hierarchical
  • Represents M:N and any relationship without duplication (hierarchical needs copies).
  • Declarative SQL and ad-hoc queries along any path; hierarchical only along the fixed tree path.
  • High physical and logical data independence; hierarchical programs depend on the tree structure.
  • No insertion/deletion anomalies from parent–child dependence (a child can exist without a parent record; FK actions are controlled).
  • Strong theory (normalization), declarative constraints, standard SQL.
  • Joins make naturally hierarchical, top-down access slower (pointer traversal in IMS is very fast).
  • Needs more processing power and memory; optimiser adds overhead.
  • Recursive tree queries (all descendants) need recursive SQL — awkward compared with a tree.
  • For a fixed, high-volume 1:N workload (e.g. bank transaction processing on IMS), hierarchical can give higher throughput.

Overall, the relational model’s flexibility, independence and query power outweigh the hierarchical model’s speed on fixed tree-shaped access.
