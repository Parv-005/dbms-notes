4Structural constraints: cardinality and participation

A relationship type usually has rules that limit which combinations of entities may appear in its relationship set. The two kinds of rule together are called the structural constraints of the relationship:

  1. the cardinality ratio (mapping cardinality): the maximum number of relationship instances an entity can take part in;
  2. the participation constraint: the minimum — must every entity take part, or may some stay out?

4.1 Cardinality ratio (mapping cardinality)

Definition

The cardinality ratio of a binary relationship type specifies the maximum number of relationship instances in which an entity can participate, i.e. the number of entities of one set to which an entity of the other set can be associated. The possible ratios are 1:1, 1:N, N:1 and M:N.

How to find the ratio from an English sentence

Ask two questions: “one A can have how many B?” and “one B can have how many A?”. Write the answer to the first question next to B and the answer to the second next to A.

“A department has many employees; an employee works in only one department” → N next to EMPLOYEE, 1 next to DEPARTMENT → DEPARTMENT 1 — WORKS_FOR — N EMPLOYEE.

Significance of cardinality (asked as “explain its significance”)
  • It records a business rule (“an employee works in only one department”) that the database must enforce.
  • It decides the table design: 1:N → foreign key on the N side, no extra table; M:N → a separate table; 1:1 → foreign key on either side, or merge (section 8). A wrong ratio gives either redundant tables or a schema that cannot store valid data.
  • It fixes the keys of relationship tables and so prevents invalid data (a second department for the same employee is rejected by the PK/UNIQUE).
  • It helps query writers know whether a join returns one row or many.

4.2 Participation constraint

Definition

The participation constraint specifies whether the existence of an entity depends on its being related to another entity through the relationship, i.e. the minimum number of relationship instances each entity must take part in.

  • Total participation (existence dependency): every entity of the set must participate in at least one relationship instance. Drawn as a double line.
  • Partial participation: some entities may not participate. Drawn as a single line.
{{svg:participation}} {{erm:t_part|Every department must have a manager (total, double line); most employees manage nothing (partial, single line). MANAGES is also 1:1.}}
Words that signal participation

Total: “every”, “each … must”, “cannot exist without”, “is always”, “at least one”. Partial: “may”, “some”, “may or may not”, “optionally”, “can have”.

Summer mid-sem example: “Every patient must undergo treatment” → PATIENT total; “a department may or may not have doctors” → DEPARTMENT partial.

4.3 (min, max) notation

Cardinality ratio and participation can be written together as a pair (min, max) on each line, with 0 ≤ min ≤ max and max ≥ 1.

Meaning

(min, max) on the line between entity type E and relationship R means: each entity e of E takes part in at least min and at most max relationship instances of R. min = 0 ⇒ partial participation; min ≥ 1 ⇒ total participation. max = 1 ⇒ e is related to at most one other entity.

{{er:t_minmax|The same schema in (min,max) notation: an employee manages 0 or 1 department, a department has exactly 1 manager; an employee works for exactly one department, a department has at least one employee.}}
Common mistake — the numbers sit on opposite sides

In the 1/N notation the N of WORKS_FOR is written next to EMPLOYEE (many employees per department). In (min,max) notation EMPLOYEE gets (1,1), because each employee takes part in exactly one WORKS_FOR, and DEPARTMENT gets (1,N). So to convert, swap sides: max next to E in (min,max) = the ratio label written next to the other entity.

Rule that works for both arrow styles

Look at where the arrowhead touches. Touching an entity (Korth) → that entity is the “1” side. Touching a diamond (Ramakrishnan) → the entity at the tail is the “many” side (each of its entities has at most one partner). Either way, in the 2023 diagram “A → R1 — B” means each A has at most one B, so A gets B’s key as a foreign key. If a diagram mixes styles or has no marks, state your assumption — the paper explicitly allows “missing data may be suitably assumed”.

4.5 Constraints on ternary relationships

For a relationship of degree 3 or more, the label next to an entity says how many of that entity can be associated with one fixed combination of the other entities.

{{er:r9|INSTRUCTOR teaches COURSE to STUDENT. 1 next to INSTRUCTOR: for a fixed (course, student) pair there is at most one instructor.}}
  • Fix (course, student): at most 1 instructor (the “1”) — a student takes a given course from one instructor.
  • Fix (instructor, student): many courses (N) — one instructor may teach the same student several courses.
  • Fix (instructor, course): many students (M) — a class has many students.
  • Consequence for tables: the key of TEACHES is made of the keys of the non-“1” sides: (course_id, student_id). Section 8, rule 9.
(min,max) on ternary relationships is different

(min,max) next to E in a ternary relationship counts how many instances each single entity e takes part in, not per pair. The two notations are not interchangeable for n-ary relationships — say which one you are using.

4.6 Ternary relationship vs three binary relationships

Can SUPPLY(supplier, part, project) be replaced by three binary relationships CAN_SUPPLY(s,p), USES(p,j), SUPPLIES(s,j)? In general, no.

{{er:t_3bin|Three binary relationships among the same entity types.}} {{svg:ternary_vs_binary}}
  • The ternary relationship stores which supplier supplied which part to which project. The binaries store only “S1 can supply P1”, “J1 uses P1”, “S1 supplies something to J1”; combining them may claim a triple that never happened.
  • The binaries may still be useful in addition (e.g. CAN_SUPPLY records suppliers who are able to supply a part even if no project has ordered it).
  • Some ternaries can be replaced: if the relationship is really two independent facts, or by turning the ternary into a (weak) entity with three binary identifying relationships — this is how ternaries are represented in models that only allow binary relationships (UML, crow’s-foot).
