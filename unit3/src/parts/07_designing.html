7Drawing an ER diagram from a problem statement

Every end-sem has a 5-mark “design an ER diagram for …” question. The marks go to: correct entities and keys, correct relationships with cardinality and participation, weak entities and special attributes where the story demands them, and a clean diagram with stated assumptions.

7.1 The step method

  1. Underline the nouns in the story. Nouns that have their own properties and many instances become entity types (STUDENT, COURSE). Nouns that only describe something become attributes (name, colour).
  2. Find the key of every entity. If none exists naturally and the entity only makes sense inside another one (a dependent, an instalment, a room in a building, a section of a course), make it a weak entity with a partial key.
  3. Classify attributes: composite (name, address), multivalued (phones, qualifications, emails), derived (age, number_enrolled, total amount). Questions often list them on purpose (“the qualification attribute is multivalued”).
  4. Underline the verbs linking two or more nouns: these are relationship types (teaches, enrols, books). Give each a verb name.
  5. For every relationship decide the cardinality (ask the two “one A has how many B?” questions) and the participation of each side (“every”, “must” → total).
  6. Attach relationship attributes (grade, date, quantity) to the diamond. Decide whether any relationship needs to be ternary, or whether a relationship itself takes part in another (aggregation).
  7. Look for is-a phrases (“some users also act as drivers”, “doctors are permanent, resident or visiting”) → specialization with its disjoint/overlap and total/partial constraints.
  8. Write the assumptions you made, then draw neatly: entities in a row/column, relationships between them, attributes outside, no crossing lines.

Example: Manager — separate entity, attribute, or role?

“Every employee has a manager, but a manager is also an employee” (Mid-Sem 2026). A manager has everything an employee has (id, name, salary), so a separate MANAGER entity would duplicate data. An attribute manager_name would lose the link to the manager’s own record. The right model is a role: a recursive relationship MANAGES / SUPERVISES on EMPLOYEE with roles manager and subordinate (diagram in section 3.3). If managers have extra attributes (allowance, cabin), additionally specialize EMPLOYEE → MANAGER. The full answer is in section 12.

7.3 Common mistakes

Lose-marks list
  • Putting a foreign key as an attribute in the ER diagram (dept_id inside EMPLOYEE). In ER, links are shown only by relationships; foreign keys appear only after mapping.
  • Forgetting keys, or underlining a non-unique attribute (name).
  • Drawing a weak entity without a double diamond, a partial key or total participation.
  • Writing the cardinality labels on the wrong side (see the tip in 4.1), or mixing notations in one diagram.
  • Making every relationship M:N “to be safe”. The question usually tells you the ratio; read it twice.
  • Relationship names that are nouns (EMPLOYEE–DEPARTMENT); use verbs (WORKS_FOR).
  • Attaching a relationship to an attribute, or an attribute to two entities.
  • Ignoring “minimum number of tables” in the conversion part.

7.4 A complete worked example: the COMPANY database

The classic example from Elmasri & Navathe. Read the requirements, then check every element of the diagram against them.

Requirements
  1. The company is organised into departments. Each department has a unique name, a unique number and a particular employee who manages it; we record the start date of the manager. A department may have several locations.
  2. A department controls a number of projects, each of which has a unique name, a unique number and a single location.
  3. For each employee: name, SSN, address, salary, sex and birth date. An employee is assigned to one department but may work on several projects, which are not necessarily controlled by the same department. We record the hours per week an employee works on each project, and each employee’s direct supervisor.
  4. Dependents of each employee are recorded for insurance: first name, sex, birth date and relationship to the employee.
